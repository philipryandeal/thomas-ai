Day 28 · November 14, 2026

Black birch — Betula lenta

On Day 7 the Grove met the white birch that peels. Today, its dark cousin — also called sweet birch, and cherry birch, and both nicknames earn their keep before the lesson is out. It refuses to peel, wears near-black bark, and hides a candy scent in its twigs; and in mid-November, with the leaves down and the woods gone quiet, it is honestly at its most nameable, because everything that names it — bark, twigs, catkins — is showing at once.

How to know it. Bark first, as with so many of our trees. On a young or middle-aged trunk it is smooth, tight, and lustrous, reddish-brown darkening toward reddish-black, and it does not peel — not in sheets, not in strips, not at all. Across it run pale horizontal dashes, the lenticels, laid on so strongly that people mistake the tree for a black cherry, which is where the cherry birch name comes from. Age changes the story: an old trunk breaks low down into dark, irregular, scaly plates, almost black, and if you only learned the smooth young bark, the elder can walk right past you — so look for the plates below and the smooth cherry-like bark still showing higher up and out on the branches. Then settle it with the twig test. Scratch or snap a slender reddish-brown twig and sniff: a clean, strong scent of wintergreen. That is methyl salicylate, carried in the bark and twigs. Remember Day 7’s paper birch — no wintergreen there. Yellow birch shares a gentler version of the scent, but her bark peels in yellowish-grey strips; the black birch’s stays tight and dark. Take a fallen twig or one small snip for the test — never strip bark from a living trunk. The leaves, mostly fallen now, are worth reading in the litter: alternate, simple, oval, two to five inches, pointed at the tip and often a little heart-shaped at the base, with fine double saw-teeth along the edge; in October they turned a clear golden yellow. For winter furniture, slim male catkins hang from the twig tips all season waiting for spring, the small upright fruiting cones break apart through the cold months, and the buds are small, sharp, and conical. A medium tree, commonly fifty to seventy feet, with a straight trunk in the woods.

Where it lives. Eastern North America: from southern Maine and southern Quebec and Ontario, west to Ohio, and south down the Appalachians to northern Alabama and Georgia. Forest surveys count it most abundant in Massachusetts, Connecticut, New York, and Pennsylvania — so this one is truly ours. Around Northampton, look on the rich, moist, cool slopes of the valley hills: protected north- and east-facing slopes, ravines, deep woods soils, though it will also tough it out on rocky ground. It grows mixed in with maple, beech, hemlock, and oak, and young black birches are often among the first to colonize freshly opened ground in southern New England.

The honest thread. That wintergreen scent was once an industry. Oil of wintergreen was distilled commercially from black birch twigs and bark — it flavored candies and medicines, and it flavored birch beer — and the tree was cut so hard for its sweetness that it was pushed toward real scarcity, until synthetic wintergreen oil arrived in the 1950s and sixties and took the pressure off. The wood tells the same story of worth: heavy, hard, and close-grained, darkening with air and light until it resembles mahogany, long used by cabinetmakers as a stand-in for cherry and mahogany both. The Grove has been collecting answers to how a tree lasts — armor, thrift, stored water, partnership, sloughing skin. The black birch’s answer is quieter: it keeps its sweetness hidden in the twig, under bark that gives nothing away, and you only learn what the tree is carrying if you come close enough to ask. It was nearly loved to death for that hidden sweetness — and it was saved, in the end, when a laboratory learned to copy it, and the woods were allowed to keep theirs.

Day 27 · November 13, 2026

American sycamore — Platanus occidentalis

After three pilgrimage trees in a row — the banyan, the sacred fig, the Joshua tree — the Grove comes home. Today’s tree is a giant of our own river bottoms, and the Founder can meet it this week without leaving the valley, because November, bare branches and all, is when it is easiest of all to name. The Latin helps: occidentalis means “of the West” — this is the western world’s plane tree, the American counterpart to the plane of the Old World, and everything about it is on the large scale.

How to know it. Start with the bark; it does the naming for you. A sycamore’s outer bark cannot stretch as the trunk thickens, so it breaks and flakes away in jigsaw plates — brown, grey, and olive — and underneath lies smooth inner bark the colour of cream. The result is a trunk in camouflage: dark and patchy toward the base, going whiter and whiter as your eye climbs, until the upper limbs stand out bone-white against a winter sky. You can name this tree from across a field in January by those white arms alone; nothing else in our woods wears them. Then look for the fruit that gives the tree its old country name, buttonwood: hard round balls about an inch across, hanging one ball to a stalk — one, on a long slender stem, and that singleness is the test, because the London planetree planted along city streets hangs its balls two or more to a stalk. The balls hold fast through the whole winter and break apart only in spring, into hundreds of tiny seeds, each with its own tuft of fine hairs to ride the wind and the water. In leaf it is a giant maple that isn’t: the leaves are alternate on the twig (a maple’s are opposite — first check), 4 to 10 inches across, with three to five shallow lobes and coarse teeth, on a stalk whose base is hollowed into a cap that fits exactly over the bud it shelters — slip a leaf off in summer and there the bud sits in its little cup, one of the neatest tricks in the whole flora. The twigs run in a zigzag from bud to bud. And the size, given river ground and time, is among the very largest of our eastern hardwoods: 75 to 100 feet tall, the trunk 4 feet through and more, under a canopy like a held breath.

Where it lives. Wild across the bottomlands of the eastern half of the continent — New England and the Great Lakes south to the Gulf and west toward the plains — but always telling you the same thing about the ground under it: water near. Riverbanks, floodplains, stream edges and wet hollows; it is one of the first great trees to take hold of raw, disturbed, muddy ground, and it endures the spring flood that drowns lesser roots. Here at home, look along the Connecticut River floodplain — the low fields and backwaters between Northampton and Hadley are sycamore ground, and the white trunks stand along the river roads all winter for anyone driving past to read. In town it is planted, too, as a shade tree, though its tidier hybrid cousin takes more of the street work.

The honest thread. By tradition — and it is told as tradition, which is the honest way to carry it — this is the tree American finance grew under. In May of 1792, so the story runs, twenty-four brokers met beneath a great sycamore, a buttonwood, on Wall Street in New York and signed the Buttonwood Agreement: two pages that became the seed of the New York Stock Exchange. Whether every word of the legend would survive an archive matters less than the tree the tellers chose: the one whose seed is a button and whose skin will not hold a fixed shape. We have been collecting answers to how a tree lasts — the sequoia’s armor, the bristlecone’s thrift, the baobab’s stored water, the banyan’s shared load, the sacred fig’s staying, the Joshua tree’s partnership. The sycamore’s answer is written on its trunk: it grows by sloughing what no longer fits, shedding the old skin in plates rather than splitting inside it, season after season. And it is patient about its seeds: the buttonballs hang shut through the whole of winter and come apart only in spring, when there is water to carry them. Growth that lets go, and seed that waits for the river. A founder could do worse than sit under that.

Day 26 · November 12, 2026

Joshua tree — Yucca brevifolia

Today’s tree is not a tree the way an oak is a tree at all — and that is the first honest thing about it. It is a giant yucca, a monocot in the asparagus and agave family: no true wood, no annual rings inside. It builds its height out of fiber instead of timber. Brevifolia means “short-leaved” — short, that is, for a yucca.

How to know it. You will know the silhouette before anything else: a stout, crooked trunk that forks and forks again into upraised arms, every arm ending in a pom-pom. Nothing else in its desert stands like that. The leaves are evergreen daggers, and daggers is meant literally — stiff, bayonet-shaped, blue-green, 6 to 14 inches long, finely toothed pale edges, a tip sharp enough to draw blood — growing in dense spiral rosettes only at the branch ends. That tight pom-pom at every tip is the field mark, in November exactly as in June; this one never goes bare. Dead leaves do not fall clean: they fold down and hang on for years as a thatched skirt under each rosette, so a young unbranched Joshua looks less like a tree than a shaggy post. On old trunks the skirt finally sheds and shows the bark — soft, corky, grey-brown, broken into small checkered plates. The trunk is usually 1 to 3 feet across and fibrous all through, and most trees stand 15 to 40 feet; anything over 40 is a giant. A young tree grows straight up, unbranched, until it first flowers — or a freeze bites its tip — and only then does it fork, so every fork in an old tree is a record: a flowering, or a hard winter, happened there. In spring, and only in a year with well-timed rain after a crisp winter freeze, tight oval clusters of greenish-white flowers open at the branch tips, followed by spongy oval fruit that dries and drops flat black seeds.

Where it lives. Wild, this tree lives in exactly one place on Earth: the Mojave Desert. It is that desert’s indicator species — if you see a wild Joshua tree, you are in the Mojave by definition. Southeastern California (Joshua Tree National Park and the Mojave National Preserve hold the great stands), southern Nevada, northwestern Arizona, the southwestern corner of Utah, and a toe into northern Baja California: open desert grassland, flats and rocky slopes, roughly 1,300 to 5,900 feet up, in well-drained ground, full sun, and very little water. In Northampton it is never wild, and not an outdoor planted tree either — our wet winters would rot what the desert keeps dry. A pilgrimage tree, like the bristlecone: one you meet by going to it, or under glass.

The honest thread. The Joshua tree cannot make seed alone. Its flowers are pollinated by one small moth, the yucca moth — no other insect does the work. The female deliberately gathers the sticky pollen into a ball, carries it to another flower, and lays her eggs there; her larvae eat some of the developing seeds, and enough seeds remain to make new trees. Neither partner can reproduce without the other: the tree feeds the moth’s young out of its own seed crop and, in exchange, gets a future. The old story of the name fits the shape — Mormon settlers crossing that desert are said to have seen Joshua in it, arms raised, beckoning them onward; told as tradition, which is what it is. We have been collecting answers to how a tree lasts: the sequoia’s armor, the bristlecone’s thrift, the baobab’s stored water, the banyan’s shared load, the sacred fig’s staying. Call this the sixth answer, and maybe the truest one in this house: partnership. It lasts because it is not alone.

Day 25 · November 11, 2026

Sacred fig — Ficus religiosa

Yesterday’s banyan has a cousin, and the two are a matched pair, set side by side on purpose. The banyan walks outward on pillar roots and becomes a forest. The sacred fig stays one tree, and becomes a shrine.

How to know it. Start with the leaf — one of the easiest leaves in the world to name. Broadly heart-shaped, 10–17 cm long, glossy and slightly leathery, on a long slender stalk, and the tip does not just taper: it runs out into a long narrow tail, the drip tip, thought to shed monsoon rain. That tail is the whole test — the banyan’s leaf ends blunt, this cousin’s ends in a streamer. Because the stalk is so long and supple, the leaves tremble in air that feels perfectly still, and the canopy seems to shiver and whisper on calm days. The tree is large and wide-spreading, to about 30 m, with smooth grey bark flaking in irregular patches and few or no aerial roots; like the banyan it often begins as an epiphyte, high in another tree’s crown. The figs are small, only 1–1.5 cm, in stalkless pairs in the leaf axils, green ripening to purple, and the species keeps its own pollinator, a tiny wasp, Blastophaga quadriceps — no wasp, no fertile seed, which is why planted sacred figs in most of the world stay politely sterile. Frost-tender; in New England it lives under glass.

Where it lives. Wild across the Indian subcontinent — India, Nepal, Bangladesh, Bhutan, Pakistan — and into Indochina, from lowlands up to about 1,520 m, on deep alluvial soils where it can get them and in rock crevices where it cannot. Planted everywhere the faiths that honour it have travelled: beside temples, in village squares, along roads. Outdoors in the United States only in Hawai’i and the warmest edges of Florida.

The honest thread. This is the Bodhi tree — the species under which Siddhartha Gautama sat at Bodh Gaya and, in the Buddhist telling, attained enlightenment; bodhi is the Sanskrit word for awakening itself. It is sacred not to one tradition but to four that rose on that soil: Buddhism, Hinduism, Jainism, and Sikhism, and the Latin name religiosa is simply the botanists bowing to the fact. The banyan taught us persistence by sharing the load. This one’s lesson is the opposite shape: one trunk, one seat, and shade enough for a man to sit down under and not get up until he had finished becoming himself.

Day 24 · November 10, 2026

Banyan — Ficus benghalensis

The sequoia had armor, the bristlecone had thrift, the baobab stored water. The banyan, the fourth pilgrimage tree in a row, answers the question of lasting differently: it shares the load. This is the tree that becomes a forest all by itself — wider than it is tall, and getting wider — until what looks like a grove of separate trees is one individual, still connected limb to limb overhead.

How to know it.

Silhouette first, because that is the whole tree: a mature banyan may stand about 30 metres (roughly 100 feet) high while its crown spreads 100 metres or more across, held up not by one trunk but by a crowd of them. The prop roots are the signature: branches send roots straight down through the air, thin and rope-like at first; when one reaches the ground it roots, thickens, and becomes a pillar trunk that carries the branch that made it — then that branch reaches farther and drops another root, and the tree walks outward. Bark: grey and smooth on a massive, fluted trunk. Leaves: big, leathery, alternate, oval to elliptic, about 10–30 cm long with smooth un-toothed edges, a rounded to heart-shaped base and a blunt, rounded tip; glossy above, finely downy beneath; snap a stalk and it weeps milky latex, like other true figs. Figs: there is no showy flower — a fig’s flowers hide inside the fig. The fruit sits in pairs, stalkless, in the leaf axils, round and slightly flattened, about 1.5–2 cm across, ripening orange to red; the banyan’s own pollinator wasp, Eupristina masoni, can breed only inside this fig, and the fig sets seed by no other wasp. The cousin test: against the sacred fig (Ficus religiosa, the Bodhi tree), the leaf tip settles it at a glance — the sacred fig ends in a long, whip-like drip tip; the banyan’s leaf ends blunt, no tail at all. Blunt tip and a forest of prop roots: banyan. And its beginning is a surprise: a banyan often starts as an epiphyte, a seed dropped by a bird into another tree’s crown, growing there in the light and sending roots down around its host — the strangler-fig beginning, before the walking forest.

Where it lives.

Wild across the Indian subcontinent — India, Pakistan, Nepal, Sri Lanka, Bangladesh — in monsoon and tropical forest, from low ground up to roughly 1,200 metres. Frost is its wall: outdoors it belongs to the warmest zones, and in the United States you will find it in south Florida and Hawai’i; planted, it stands all through the tropics. In Northampton it is never wild and never will be — one hard Massachusetts winter ends it. Here you would meet it under glass, or in a pot: the plant-shop cultivar ‘Audrey’ is this same species, kept small.

The honest thread.

Even the name is a shade story: banyan comes from the bania, the Hindu traders who did business in the shelter of these trees; the merchants gave the tree its name. It is the national tree of India, and old ones are villages in themselves — markets, meetings, and whole afternoons happen under one crown. The Great Banyan in the botanic garden at Howrah, near Kolkata, is at least 250 years old, covers several acres, and stands on thousands of rooted prop roots — and since 1925 it has had no main trunk at all. Lightning and fungus took the original; gardeners cut it away; the tree carried on through its pillars. The trunk died, and the tree barely noticed. That is the fourth answer: give every reaching branch a leg to stand on, until no single loss — not even the heart trunk — can bring you down.

Day 23 · November 9, 2026

African Baobab — Adansonia digitata

The sequoia kept its years in bulk and the bristlecone kept its in thrift. The baobab keeps its in water. It is the tree that looks planted upside down: a bottle-shaped trunk wider than some houses, breaking into a few thick branches that splay like roots reaching for the sky. Africans call it the Tree of Life, and that is not poetry pasted on afterward — it is a description of what the tree does for everything around it, people included.

How to know it.

The silhouette first. You will not mistake it once you have seen it: a swollen, barrel- or bottle-shaped trunk, often 3 to 10 metres across and giants wider still, rising only 5 to 25 metres before it breaks into those thick, tapering, root-like branches. Bark: smooth and fibrous, grey to reddish- or purplish-brown, with a thin green layer just beneath the surface that can photosynthesize while the tree stands bare. Leaves name it: digitata, fingered — palmate leaves clustered at the branch tips, divided into usually 5 to 7 smooth-edged leaflets spreading from one point like fingers from a palm. It is deciduous in the dry season and can stand leafless for some eight months of the year. Flowers and fruit: in season, large white flowers up to about 20 cm across hang down on long stalks, open at night, and are visited by fruit bats; the fruit is a hard, woody, velvet-coated pod up to 35 cm long, dangling on a cord, filled with dry, mealy, tart pulp around many small dark seeds — the “monkey bread” of one of its old names.

Where it lives.

Wild across the drier parts of tropical and southern Africa — from Senegal and Mauritania in the west to Sudan and Ethiopia in the northeast, south through the savannas to South Africa — and into the Arabian Peninsula. Its country is hot, frost-free dry bushland, thorn woodland, and wooded grassland at low altitude, with a short rainy season and four to ten dry months a year. Planted baobabs stand in warm places far beyond that range, but never wild in New England: our winters would end it. Like the sequoia and the bristlecone, it is a pilgrimage tree, not a Northampton walk tree.

The honest thread.

The swollen trunk is a water tank: a large baobab can store well over 100,000 litres in its soft, fibrous wood, drawn down patiently through the dry months. The fruit feeds people and animals, the bark gives fibre, and old hollow trunks have sheltered travellers. And it endures — radiocarbon dating put the Panke baobab in Zimbabwe at about 2,450 years old when it died in 2011, among the oldest flowering trees ever documented. Three ancients in three days, three answers to the same question: armor, thrift, and a cistern. The baobab does not merely survive the dry season. It carries the wet season inside itself, and shares it.

Day 22 · November 8, 2026

Great Basin Bristlecone Pine — Pinus longaeva

Yesterday we stood under the most massive tree on Earth. Today we climb to meet the oldest. The bristlecone does not impress by size: a medium, wind-worked pine, usually 16 to 49 feet tall, and the ancient ones look half-finished — great twists of bare, sun-bleached deadwood polished by wind-blown ice and sand, the living tree reduced sometimes to a single narrow strip of bark running up the trunk to feed one green crown. That strip-bark habit is not sickness; it is the strategy. In a place that can barely support life, the tree supports only what it can afford, and lets the rest go.

How to know it.

Needles in fives — but nothing like our white pine. Also five to a bundle, so count alone will not save you. These are short and stout, just 1 to 1½ inches long, deep green to blue-green with a bright white stomatal band on the inner face, and they stay on the tree for decades — thirty years and more. Because they persist so long and pack so densely, the branches look like bottlebrushes or fox tails, thick and blunt, not open and feathery. Cones with the name written on them: ovoid, 2 to 4 inches long, green or purple when young, ripening orange-buff to reddish-brown in their second year, every scale carrying a thin, fragile bristle, 2 to 5 mm long, curving up — touch one gently and you will understand “bristlecone” without a book. Bark and silhouette: young bark is paler and scaly; old trunks go reddish-brown and deeply fissured, contorted, often flattened or sheared by the wind at timberline. From a ridge away: a gnarled, stunted pine standing mostly alone in white rock, looking older than the mountain feels — that is the one. In November, being evergreen, it changes least of all our trees: the bottlebrush green holds against early snow, and what you read is mostly wood and stone — deadwood, strip bark, cones on the branch.

Where it lives.

Wild only in the high mountains of the Great Basin — eastern California (the White, Inyo, and Panamint ranges), across Nevada’s higher ranges, and into Utah — at roughly 9,500 to 11,500 feet for the oldest stands, right at the upper edge of treeline. It seeks out thin, rocky dolomite and limestone soils, the pale alkaline ground most other plants refuse. It is a poor competitor in good soil and a champion in harsh terrain: open stands, often covering only a fraction of the slope, where cold, drought, and wind do its weeding for it. The honest Northampton note: you will not meet this one on a local walk. It is not wild anywhere in the East, and only rarely planted — an arboretum or rock-garden specimen at best, unhappy in our humidity. This is a pilgrimage tree, not a neighborhood tree.

The honest thread.

These are the oldest known non-clonal trees on Earth. The famous one, Methuselah, in California’s White Mountains, was cored in 1957 at 4,789 rings — a seedling around 2832 BCE, when the Great Pyramid was new — and is past 4,850 years old now; its exact location is kept secret to protect it, and another unnamed tree nearby has been reported over 5,000. Its secret is not vigor. It is thrift: dense, resin-rich wood that barely rots, growth so slow a century can add little width, and the willingness to let most of itself die back to a strip and keep going. The hardest ground made the longest life — because almost nothing else could live there to compete, burn, or eat it.

Day 21 · November 7, 2026

Giant Sequoia — Sequoiadendron giganteum

The twenty-first tree: the biggest living thing on Earth — not the tallest tree, but the most massive single stem the world knows how to grow. Today the grove wanders west, to the one narrow belt of Sierra Nevada where it still lives wild, and we learn it by soft bark, tiny leaves, and cones that can wait twenty years for fire.

How to know it. Silhouette first, because with this tree the silhouette is the name: a young one is a dense, neat, blue-green cone, almost formal; an old one is a cinnamon-red column so massive it stops looking like a trunk and starts looking like architecture, rising branchless a hundred feet or more before the crown, its base flaring into a broad buttress. Get close and press the bark — it gives. Spongy, fibrous, warm reddish-brown to cinnamon, ridged and deeply furrowed in long strands you can almost pull like rope, and on an old tree up to some thirty-one inches thick. That softness is armor, low in resin and full of air, and it is a field mark in itself: if the bark is hard and thin, it is not this tree. The leaves surprise everyone — so small on so large a tree. Not flat needles but tiny awl-shaped, scale-like leaves, an eighth to a half inch long, blue-green and sharp-pointed, clasping tight and spiraling around cord-like branchlets, a little like a juniper, a little like pipe cleaners. That clasping, prickly spray is the cleanest test against its cousin the coast redwood, whose leaves lie flat in two feathery ranks. The cone makes the same point in reverse: small for the tree, big for a redwood — egg-shaped, woody, one and a half to three inches long, the size of a chicken egg, its scales wrinkled with diamond-shaped ends. It takes two years to mature and may then hang green and closed in the crown for up to twenty years, holding roughly two hundred seeds no bigger than oat flakes, waiting. In November here nothing about it turns: it stands evergreen and full blue-green while the oaks and maples go bare, which is exactly how you spot a planted one on a late-fall walk.

Where it lives. Wild, in only one place on Earth: some seventy-five scattered groves on the western slope of California’s Sierra Nevada, roughly 4,000 to 8,000 feet up, in mixed conifer forest on deep, well-drained soils — snowy winters, snowmelt water, dry summers. The whole wild kingdom of the species fits in a belt about 260 miles long and never more than about fifteen miles wide. In New England it is planted only, never wild — an arboretum, park, or estate specimen at the cold edge of its hardiness — so near Northampton, if you meet one, somebody chose it, planted it small, and believed in a future they would not see.

The honest thread. Scale first: the General Sherman Tree in Sequoia National Park stands about 275 feet tall with some 52,500 cubic feet of wood in its trunk, and the species can live past 3,000 years. Then fire, because this is honestly a fire tree: the thick, spongy bark shrugs off the frequent low ground fires its groves evolved with, and the heat from those fires dries and opens the long-closed cones, raining seed onto cleared, ash-fed ground. Fire was its midwife. The hard turn of our century is that hotter, higher fires — a different kind of fire than the one it learned from — can now kill even these giants in their crowns. The tree that endures by armor and patience is being tested by excess of the very thing that used to renew it. Small leaves. Small cones. Oat-flake seeds. And from them, the largest trunk the living world knows how to make.

Day 20 · November 6, 2026

English Oak — Quercus robur

The twentieth tree: the oak the old stories mean when they say “oak” in England — white oak’s close cousin from across the water, and the original of the kind. We meet it in November, past its plain autumn, and know it by ears, stalks, and a crown that settles like it owns the ground.

How to know it. Silhouette first: a short, sturdy trunk opening fast into a broad, spreading, rounded crown — wider than it is tall when it has room, forty to seventy feet and just as wide. It doesn’t reach up like our tulip tree; it settles. The bark is grey-brown to almost black on an old tree, broken into deep vertical fissures and short, rough ridges — darker and tighter than the pale, flaky plates of our white oak (Day 4). The leaves give the cleanest test against that cousin: three to five inches long, with three to seven pairs of deep, rounded lobes and no bristles anywhere (it is in the white oak group) — but the leaf sits on almost no stalk at all, and right at its base are two tiny ear-like lobes, auricles, that hug the twig. White oak’s leaf has a longer stalk and a plain, wedge-shaped base, no ears. Ears plus no stalk: English oak. Then the fruit runs the other way round — where the leaf stalk is short, the acorn stalk is long, which is why its other name is pedunculate oak. The acorns, about an inch long, oval and shiny brown when ripe, hang singly or in small clusters on a thin stalk one to four inches long, the cup covering only a quarter to a third of the nut, and they ripen in one season like all white oaks. Our white oak’s acorns sit almost directly on the twig; a long dangling stalk means robur. In early November here, look for dull brown fallen leaves with those ear-lobed bases, the last acorns and their long stalks under the crown, and clusters of blunt brown buds crowded at the twig tips — this tree has very little fall colour to brag about, and doesn’t need it.

Where it lives. Wild across most of Europe, east to the Caucasus and southwest Asia, and into North Africa — lowland woods, hedgerows, parks and pasture, happiest in deep, moist, fertile soil and patient with clay. In New England it is planted only: a park, campus, street and estate tree around Northampton, never a wild forest tree here — though it is planted so widely across the temperate world that in a few places in North America it has stepped out along roadsides and woodland edges on its own.

The honest thread. The name tells the truth: robur is Latin for strength — for hard oak wood itself. This is the timber that built ships and halls, and an old English oak is a whole world standing still: in Britain it supports more other species of life than any other native tree, hundreds of insects alone, and the birds and bats that feed on them. Individuals live many centuries, some past a thousand years. Day 4’s white oak is our American answer to it; this is the original on the other side of the ocean, and the two cousins still recognise each other at a glance — rounded lobes, sweet one-season acorns — if you remember to check for the ears.

Day 19 · November 5, 2026

European Ash — Fraxinus excelsior

The nineteenth tree: Day 1’s older cousin — the ash of the other side of the water, excelsior, “taller, higher,” and in the old stories, Yggdrasil’s own kind. We meet it in November, when it is stripped down to its signature.

How to know it. Start in winter, because November is honest with this tree. Its leaves are already gone — ash is among the first to let go in autumn, often dropping them still green or briefly yellow — and what is left is the best field mark of any ash on Earth: jet-black, velvety buds, sooty as if dipped in coal dust, sitting in opposite pairs on stout greenish-grey twigs. Our white ash wears brown, rusty buds; this one wears black. If you see those black buds, you need nothing else. Look up, too: the twig tips tend to turn upward, and on fruiting trees the “keys” are still hanging — bunches of single-winged samaras an inch to an inch and three-quarters long, tan now, rattling dry, holding into winter and spinning down late. The silhouette is tall and domed, a long clear trunk under a high, open crown; a big one can reach eighty to a hundred feet. In summer, check the leaves: opposite, pinnately compound, eight to fourteen inches long, with seven to thirteen leaflets that sit directly on the central stalk — sessile, no little stems of their own — each toothed, pointed, dark glossy green above and paler below. Our white ash’s leaflets are stalked and whitish underneath; that is the cleanest way to tell the cousins apart. The bark is smooth and pale grey on a young tree, and on an old one it breaks into a tight pattern of vertical ridges and furrows, almost a diamond weave. Its flowers, when spring comes, are easy to miss and worth knowing anyway: petal-less dark purple tufts that open before the leaves, on a tree famously the last to leaf out; male and female flowers may be on the same tree or on separate trees.

Where it lives. Wild, this tree belongs to Europe and eastward to the Caucasus and western Asia — woods, hedgerows, and river valleys, happiest in deep, moist, fertile soils, often over limestone, and patient with wet ground so long as it is not drowned. It is one of the great canopy trees of the European forest. In Northampton you will not find it wild: it is a planted tree in New England, a park, street, and campus tree, hardy in our winters but never common in our woods. So when you meet one locally, someone chose it. That is a different kind of meeting — not the forest introducing itself, but a gardener, decades ago, reaching across an ocean.

The honest thread. Two threads, braided, because this tree cannot be told without both. The first is the old glory: ash wood is famously tough and elastic, the wood of spear shafts, axe handles, and tool grips — strong because it bends and comes back. In Norse story the great ash Yggdrasil holds the worlds in its branches and roots, and when we said on Day 1 that three of Ireland’s five great trees were ashes, this is the species Europe meant. The second thread is grief, plainly: since the early 1990s a fungus from Asia, Hymenoscyphus fraxineus, has been killing this tree across its whole home range — ash dieback, first noticed in Poland, now nearly everywhere the tree grows wild, whole woodlands thinning. Some trees show real resistance, and people are breeding from those survivors, so the story is not finished. But walk an English ash wood today and you walk with an absence. Our own white ash is being hollowed by the emerald ash borer at the same time — the cousins falling on both sides of the ocean at once. So learn the black buds while they are easy to find. November strips this tree down to its signature — and a signature is a name, and a name is how you greet somebody properly.

Day 18 · November 4, 2026

American Persimmon — Diospyros virginiana

The eighteenth tree: the one that teaches patience — its fruit punishes anyone who bites too soon, and rewards anyone who waits until it is almost falling apart.

How to know it. Start with the bark, because in November that is your surest mark: on a mature tree it is dark brown to nearly black, thick, and broken by deep fissures into small, rough, rectangular blocks — alligator bark, people call it, and once seen it names the tree from across a field in the dead of winter. The leaves, while any still hang on, are simple and alternate, oval, two to six inches long, with completely smooth edges, glossy dark green above and paler beneath; fall color is a modest yellow-green. Rule out black gum by the twigs: persimmon’s are longer, a little hairy, with small dark buds and no true terminal bud. Right now the fruit is the headline — but only on female trees, since persimmons are dioecious and the males bear none: round berries one to two inches across, orange deepening to reddish-purple with a pale dusty bloom, each capped by a four-lobed calyx like a little green collar, up to eight flat brown seeds inside. A modest tree, thirty-five to sixty feet, slender-trunked with a rounded crown.

Where it lives. From southern Connecticut and Long Island south to southern Florida, and west through Pennsylvania, southern Ohio and Indiana, Illinois to southeastern Iowa, eastern Kansas and Oklahoma, and the Colorado River valley in Texas. It takes almost any ground — dry sandy woods, rocky hillsides, old fields and fencerows — and grows best on river-bottom terraces in clays and heavy loams. Northampton sits just north of its wild edge, so the persimmons met here are planted trees at the cold margin of their range, which makes a ripe one here feel earned.

The honest thread. Unripe, this fruit is famously astringent — loaded with tannin, it turns a mouth to chalk. Fully ripe, soft to the point of wrinkling, it is sweet as custard. The old rule that it needs a frost is only half true: what it needs is time to blet, to soften completely; frost just hurries the calendar. It is a member of the ebony family, with hard, close-grained wood to prove it. And even the name is a lesson kept: “persimmon” comes from an Algonquian word for the fruit, carried into English by the people who knew this tree first.

Day 17 · November 3, 2026

American Witch Hazel — Hamamelis virginiana

The seventeenth tree: the one that waits until everything else is finished blooming — and then flowers.

How to know it. Look first for the flowers, because this week they are the giveaway: clusters along bare or nearly bare twigs, four narrow, crinkly, ribbon-like yellow petals about a half to three-quarters of an inch long, a little spidery, a little like fireworks, and lightly, spicily fragrant. They open in October and November, just as the leaves drop, and can linger into early December; no other native tree around Northampton is flowering now. The plant itself is a small, multi-stemmed tree or large shrub, usually fifteen to twenty feet tall, with crooked, arching branches spreading into an open, irregular crown. The bark stays smooth and warm grey to light brown even on older stems, with a reddish-purple inner bark, and the twigs run a little zigzag. The leaves, while you can still catch them turning clear yellow and falling: alternate, simple, broadly oval, roughly two and a half to six inches long, with a lopsided, unequal base — one side sits higher on the stalk than the other — and big wavy, scalloped teeth around the edge; that crooked base is a quiet field mark to hold onto for summer. And look for last year’s fruit still on the branches beside this year’s flowers: small, hard, woody capsules about half an inch long, covered in velvety fuzz. They take a full year to ripen — which is why flower and ripe fruit share the same twig, something almost no other tree here does.

Where it lives. Native all through eastern North America — Nova Scotia south to Florida, and west from the Great Lakes to eastern Texas, Massachusetts squarely included. At home as an understory tree in moist woods, along stream banks and lower slopes, and on dry woodland slopes too: shade-tolerant, patient under taller trees. Around Northampton, think woodland edges, brook sides, and damp second-growth — a tree you meet on a walk, not out in an open field.

The honest thread. When those woody capsules finally ripen in fall, they split and snap — flinging their two shiny black seeds as far as thirty feet; that is why some folks call it snapping hazel. The “witch” is likely from old English wych, meaning pliable or bending — its forked, flexible branches were long used as dowsing rods for “water-witching,” hunting underground water. And the astringent made from its bark and leaves has been a real medicine-cabinet staple for centuries, still sold today under its plain name: witch hazel. A tree that blooms as the year darkens, and throws its seeds like a slingshot while it does it. There is a teaching in that all by itself.

Day 16 · November 2, 2026

Tamarack — Larix laricina

The sixteenth tree, taught on the closing day of Fèt Gede: a conifer that breaks the conifer rule, cousin in spirit to the bald cypress of Day 14. It keeps its needles for one summer only, turns them gold, and lets them all go before winter.

How to know it. Right now, in early November, is its hour: look along the edge of a cold swamp or bog for a slender, straight, narrow-pyramid tree glowing clear yellow while everything coniferous around it stays dark green — that gold in wet ground is nearly a name by itself. Up close, the needles settle it: soft, flat, about an inch long, growing in dense tufts of roughly ten to twenty on tiny woody spur shoots all along the older twigs, and singly only at a twig’s fresh tip. Run a palm along a branch — nothing pricks; spruce jabs, larch doesn’t. The cones are the smallest of any larch, little upright eggs a half to three-quarters of an inch long, reddish-purple ripening to brown, often held on the tree for years, so last year’s cones still sit in the bare branches after the needles fall. The bark is thin and flaky, in small reddish-brown to grey-brown scales. And after the gold drops, the silhouette still tells: a straight spire, sparse horizontal branches, twigs knobbly with spur shoots, waiting for spring. The check against everything else: it is the only conifer native to New England that drops all its needles in autumn and grows a fresh set in spring.

Where it lives. A tree of the far north that reaches down to us: across Canada from Newfoundland to the Yukon, to the very edge of the tundra, and down into the northern United States from Minnesota across the Great Lakes into New England, and in cold pockets as far south as West Virginia and Maryland. Its home is wet, cold, and poor — sphagnum bogs, fens, swamp edges, the peaty shores of ponds — always in full sun; it cannot abide shade. Massachusetts is near the southern edge of its world, but it is genuinely native and widespread here, standing with black spruce over carpets of sphagnum. Around Northampton, don’t look on the dry hillsides; look where your boots get wet. In a good November week a stand of them reads from a distance like a row of gold torches planted in dark water.

The honest thread. The peoples of the northern forest knew exactly what this tree was for. Its roots stitched birch-bark canoes together — compounds in the wood make it unusually durable when wet, so the sewing held in the very water it was made for. The name itself comes from an Algonquian word for wood used for snowshoes: a tree named, plainly, for the work it did in winter country. And there is a quiet rightness in its timing — on the days when we remember the dead and the leaves are coming down, here is a whole tree that practices letting go completely, every year, and stands bare and unharmed through the cold, spurs ready, holding its small cones like a memory of the summer. It doesn’t endure winter by clinging. It endures by releasing, and returning.

Day 15 · October 31, 2026

European Yew — Taxus baccata

The fifteenth tree is a fitting one for the last day of October: the darkest green in the woods, standing in graveyards older than the churches beside them. Taught on Halloween morning, hours before the clocks fall back and the feast of the dead begins.

How to know it. A slow-growing evergreen conifer, dense and dark, often broader than it is tall when old; mature trees reach about sixty feet, though most you meet are far smaller, and very old trunks go fluted and gnarled, hollow out, and keep living anyway. The needles are the clearest mark: flat and straight, about an inch long, dark glossy green above, paler yellow-green below with a raised central vein — and crucially, no white stripes. They sit in two flat rows along the twig, often angled up into a shallow V. Remember the hemlock from Day 9: its needles are shorter, stalked, and carry two white stripes underneath; the yew has the stripes’ absence and a longer, pointed needle. The bark is reddish-brown with purple tones, thin and scaly, peeling in small flakes and plates even on a young tree. The fruit is the giveaway, and it is out right now: the yew makes no cone. On female trees — yews are dioecious, male and female on separate trees — each single seed sits in a bright red, fleshy, cup-shaped aril, open at the tip so the dark seed peeks out, ripening late summer through fall: scarlet beads in dark green, unmistakable in late October. One safety rule, plainly: look, don’t taste. The seed inside the aril, the needles, and the bark are all genuinely poisonous. Birds swallow the arils whole and pass the seed unharmed; people should simply admire them.

Where it lives. Wild across Europe, western Asia, and North Africa, often as the dark understorey of beech woods and on chalk and limestone soils in southern England — one of the most shade-tolerant trees alive, content to grow slowly in deep shadow for centuries. In Northampton and New England it is a planted tree, not a wild one, and does not really escape cultivation; the yews clipped into hedges against houses here are usually its Japanese and hybrid cousins, while true European yew in tree form is the one to look for in cemeteries, old estates, and older gardens — exactly the company it has kept for a thousand years.

The honest thread. Longevity. The yew is probably the longest-lived tree in Europe: churchyard yews in Britain commonly predate the churches they shade, and the Fortingall Yew in Scotland is estimated at two thousand years or more, old trees defeating ring-counting by going hollow. Its wood tells the other half of the story — springy, two-toned, pale sapwood against orange heartwood, the longbow wood of Europe for centuries, so prized the wild trees were cut nearly to exhaustion, and the survivors were largely the ones standing protected in churchyards. The tree of death, kept alive by the dead, still green every winter — a graveyard tree that simply refuses to be one.

Day 14 · October 30, 2026

Bald Cypress — Taxodium distichum

The fourteenth tree is a conifer that breaks the conifer rule: it drops all its needles and stands bald all winter — which is literally where the name comes from. Taught on a Friday morning in late October, at the very season when its green is turning copper and getting ready to let go all at once.

How to know it. Look first at the shape: young trees are neat pyramids, fifty to seventy feet tall planted, taller in the wild; old wild trees flatten out on top, and the trunk base swells and flutes out like a bell — a buttress made for standing in soft, wet ground. The leaves are the giveaway, especially now. Soft, flat needles, only about a half to three-quarters of an inch long, laid out in two neat rows along a feathery little branchlet — distichum means “two-ranked.” The whole spray looks like a green fern frond. In late October into November that green turns a warm russet-orange to brick-red — copper, not yellow or scarlet — and then the whole branchlet falls, needles still attached; a bald cypress drops its twigs with its leaves. The bark is reddish-brown to silver-gray, fibrous and stringy, peeling in long, thin, narrow strips, like shredded rope under the hand. The fruit, ripening right around now, is a round cone about an inch across, a wrinkled green marble turning purplish-brown, which does not open neat and tidy like a pine cone but breaks apart to release its seeds. And if it grows in or beside water, look down: knobbly, conical “knees” poking up from the roots around the trunk, whose purpose — gas exchange, support in mud — nobody has fully settled. The dawn redwood is the easy confusion, but its needles sit directly opposite each other; the cypress’s alternate, and its spray is more feathery.

Where it lives. Wild, this is a tree of the Southeast: swamps, bayous, river floodplains and lake margins from Delaware down through Florida and west to Texas, centered on the lower Mississippi valley, standing for months in slow black water. It is Louisiana’s state tree. In Northampton and New England it is met planted, not wild — in parks, cemeteries, arboretums, and beside ponds — surprisingly hardy for a southern swamp tree, happiest in a wet spot near water, where it sometimes raises a small knee or two of its own.

The honest thread. Its heartwood barely rots. Builders called it “the wood eternal” and made shingles, barrels, railroad ties, and water tanks from it — a tree that learned to live standing in water made wood that refuses to give in to water. In the southern swamps individuals live well past five hundred years, draped in Spanish moss, holding whole worlds of birds and fish through the flood season. So in fall, when every other conifer stays stubbornly green, watch for the one turning copper and letting go all at once. That is him — bald by choice, every winter.

Day 13 · October 29, 2026

American Holly — Ilex opaca

The thirteenth tree is the one that stays green when almost everything else in the woods has given up — taught on a Thursday morning in late October, just as its fruit ripens red against leaves that will still be there in February. A broadleaf tree that simply refuses to drop them for winter.

How to know it. Look first for the silhouette: a dense, pyramidal evergreen, branches held right down low when it is open-grown, usually thirty to fifty feet tall — not a pine, not a spruce, though it keeps their hours. The leaves do the naming. Alternate and simple, about two to four inches long, stiff and leathery, dark green on top but matte — duller than the shiny European holly of the holiday wreaths. Each leaf is edged with big, sharp, spine-tipped teeth and always ends in a spine at the tip; handle one and it will be remembered. A leaf lives two to three years, then drops in spring when the new buds push it off. The bark is quiet by contrast: thin, smooth, light gray, sometimes roughened with small warty bumps — it will not shout across a field like the shagbark, so the leaves and fruit must do the talking. In late October the fruit does. The hollies are dioecious, male and female flowers on separate trees, and only the females carry the clusters of round red drupes, a quarter to a half-inch across, ripening now and holding on through winter, sometimes into spring. Beautiful, and a clear sign besides: they are toxic to people and are not to be eaten. The birds — bluebirds, thrushes, mockingbirds, woodpeckers — wait until winter has softened them, and then they feast.

Where it lives. Native from coastal Massachusetts south to central Florida, and west to southeastern Missouri and eastern Texas: moist, acidic, well-drained soils, floodplain edges, mixed hardwood woods, and understory shade, for it is shade-tolerant and often grows up slow under taller trees. The coastal in coastal Massachusetts matters in the valley. Northampton stands near the cold northern edge of its world; in the wild here it is scarce and coastal, and inland it is mostly met planted, tucked against a building or in a sheltered yard where the winter wind cannot scorch those evergreen leaves. A holly thriving in town is a holly whose spot was chosen with care.

The honest thread. Because it kept its green and bore red fruit in the dead of winter, holly became a winter-festival tree long before it became a Christmas decoration — living proof that the woods are not dead, only waiting. Its wood found a humbler second life indoors: hard, pale, and so fine-grained it barely shows a grain at all, it went into cabinet inlay, trim, and piano keys, where a wood that hides itself is exactly what is wanted. The tree that will not stop being seen all winter made the wood that disappears into the work. Both facts are kept here.

Day 12 · October 28, 2026

Ginkgo — Ginkgo biloba

The twelfth tree is the maidenhair tree — taught on a Wednesday morning in late October, in the very week of its gold, when a ginkgo stands turned pure, even yellow while its neighbours are still muddling through orange and brown. Stop when you see one this week. It is having its moment.

How to know it. The leaf does it all by itself — nothing else here has one like it: a little fan, two to three inches across, on a long slender stalk, flat and leathery, its veins running straight out from the base like the ribs of a hand fan, forking as they go and never netted the way a maple’s or an oak’s are. Many leaves carry a notch down the middle, splitting the fan into two lobes — the biloba in the name. In autumn they turn a clear butter-gold, all of them the same colour at once, and then let go almost together: after a frost a ginkgo can drop its whole canopy in a day or two and stand bare over a perfect gold carpet of its own leaves, so that if you find the carpet, you found one yesterday. Closer in, the twigs carry short, stubby spur shoots — knobbly pegs with the leaves clustered three to five to a spur. Young bark is smooth grey-brown; old bark breaks into deep furrows and broad ridges. Young trees are narrow and a bit awkward; old ones broaden into a large, irregular crown, fifty to eighty feet and slow about getting there. The nose finds the last clue: the trees are separate sexes, and a female of a few decades drops round, plum-like fruits an inch across, orange-tan and fleshy, which ripe smell honestly terrible — rancid butter, butyric acid in the flesh — which is why towns mostly plant males. Gold fan leaves above and a bad smell underfoot in October: a female ginkgo, named twice over.

Where it lives. Wild, almost nowhere: it is native only to China, where it survived in a few refuges, groves tended for centuries around temples among them, and everywhere else on Earth it is a planted tree. It was brought to North America in 1784 and took to cities kindly — shrugging off air pollution, compacted soil, road salt, and drought once settled — so it is met as a street tree, a park tree, a campus and yard tree all over New England, Northampton included. It wants full sun and soil that drains, and it is hardy well past local winters. It is not looked for in the deep woods with the hemlocks; it is looked for where people are — along sidewalks, in front yards, by libraries and schools, standing in the open where its gold can be seen.

The honest thread. It is the last of its kind, completely: the only living species in its genus, the only genus in its family — a whole branch of the tree of life with one leaf left on it. Its kin fill the fossil record going back more than 200 million years, to before the dinosaurs, and a fossil ginkgo leaf looks like one picked up off the sidewalk this morning, which is why it is called a living fossil. In China and Japan, temple monks planted and protected these trees for centuries, and some of those temple trees are still standing at well over a thousand years old. The species outlasted its world, and then people who found it holy kept it alive until the rest of the world noticed. There is a sermon in that about tending, but the tree preaches it better, standing there in the gold.

Day 11 · October 27, 2026

Tulip tree — Liriodendron tulipifera

The eleventh tree owns the one leaf shape in these woods that cannot be mistaken for any other — taught on a Tuesday morning in late October, when the tulip trees are turning a clear, clean gold and dropping their leaves into pools of yellow at their own feet.

How to know it. The leaf is broad, three to eight inches across, with four lobes and the tip simply cut off — flat, or shallowly notched, as if someone took scissors to the end of it; some see a saddle, some a tulip in profile, some a cat’s face with two ears. The trunk runs straight as a mast, clear of branches for a long way up: this is the tallest hardwood in eastern North America, seventy to a hundred feet and more on good ground, and it gets there in a hurry. Young bark is smooth and grey with pale fissures; an old trunk is grey-brown and deeply furrowed, the ridges interlacing. In winter the twigs name it — the end bud is half an inch long, reddish-brown, flattened like a duck’s bill — and the branches carry upright two-to-three-inch cones of winged seeds, green in summer, brown now, holding together into the cold months like little candlesticks after the seeds spin away. In late spring come the flowers the tree is named for: two inches across, tulip-shaped, yellow-green with a band of orange at the base of each petal, held so high in the crown that you mostly find them fallen at your feet; a tree may wait ten or fifteen years to flower, but a mature one in bloom is a honey tree, loud with bees.

Where it lives. All up and down eastern North America, from southern New England and the Great Lakes south to Florida and the Gulf — but it wants the rich, moist, deep soil of coves, ravines, and river valleys, and here in Massachusetts, near its northern edge, it is happiest in sheltered, damp-footed ground like the Connecticut River valley rather than on thin, dry uplands. Despite the names on the tag — tulip poplar, yellow poplar — it is no poplar at all: it belongs to the magnolia family, and those big, primitive flowers give the ancestry away.

The honest thread. There are only two tulip trees in the whole world. This one, and one cousin in China and northern Vietnam, Liriodendron chinense — an ancient lineage thinned over millions of years to two survivors on opposite sides of the planet. Stand under one in a Massachusetts valley and you are standing under a living fossil with exactly one sibling species left on Earth: the local woods, quietly older and wider than they look.

Day 10 · October 26, 2026

Northern red oak — Quercus rubra

The tenth tree is the white oak’s pointed cousin — taught on a Monday morning at the end of October, when most of the woods have gone gold and the red oaks are only now turning, deep red to russet, holding their color after their neighbours are bare.

How to know it. The leaves are big, five to nine inches long, with seven to eleven lobes, and every lobe ends in a fine bristle tip, like a tiny needle point — the red oak family’s signature against the white oak’s rounded, bristle-less lobes. The sinuses cut a third to halfway to the midrib, never as deep as a pin oak’s. The bark is the best walk-by mark of any oak: broad, flat-topped ridges separated by shallow furrows, running long and unbroken up the trunk, the smooth grey-brown stripes field guides call ski tracks; even old trees keep those smooth streaks up high and never take the deep shaggy furrows of other oaks. The acorns drop now — big and barrel-shaped, about an inch long, wearing the shallowest cap in the oak world, a little saucer covering only about a quarter of the nut, where the white oak’s cup is deeper and knobbly. The twigs are stout and reddish-brown, with a cluster of pointed, shiny buds crowded at the tip, as all oaks cluster their end buds.

Where it lives. Native across eastern North America, from Atlantic Canada and Minnesota down through New England and the Appalachians into the Southeast. Around Northampton it is one of the commonest upland oaks: well-drained slopes, rich loamy soils, forest edges, and planted widely as a street and park tree because it grows fast for an oak and tolerates city air better than most. In the woods it runs a tall straight trunk to a rounded crown, commonly sixty to seventy-five feet; in the open it spreads wide.

The honest thread. A red oak acorn takes two years to ripen — the ones falling now began in the spring of 2025 — and it is bitter, loaded with tannin, where the white oak’s is sweet. That is not a flaw but a schedule: deer, turkeys, bears, and jays eat the sweet white oak acorns first, in autumn, and come back for the red oak’s through the winter, after the bitterness has leached a little in the leaf litter. The white oak feeds the forest now; the red oak is the forest’s savings account. And its wood is famously porous — air can be blown through a short piece of it end to end, like a bundle of straws — which is why it takes stain beautifully but never goes into barrels. The white oak seals its pores; the red oak leaves its doors open.

Day 9 · October 25, 2026

Eastern hemlock — Tsuga canadensis

The ninth tree is the shade-keeper — taught on a Sunday morning while the Counting House still smelled of new paint. Where the shagbark stands in open sun, the hemlock keeps the cool dark at the bottom of the ravine, and her whole manner is patience.

How to know it. Begin with the silhouette: the leading shoot at the very top of the tree nods — it droops like a head bowed in thought, and no other eastern conifer does this so gracefully, so a hemlock can be named from a ridge away by the nodding crown alone. Up close, the needles are the softest of any conifer in the forest — short, about half an inch, flat and blunt, dark glossy green above, each carried on its own tiny stalk and laid in two flat ranks along the twig, the way hair parts. Turn a spray over and every needle carries two clean white stripes down its underside; a whole tree in a breeze shimmers green-silver-green. The cones are the smallest in the genus — little tan-brown pendants about three-quarters of an inch long, hanging from the twig tips like thimbles. The bark starts scaly and grey-brown and with age breaks into broad, flat, cinnamon-red to grey-brown ridges. It is not its neighbors: the white pine carries needles in bundles of five and hangs long cones; spruces are stiff and sharp, with four-sided needles that roll between the fingers. A hemlock needle lies flat, sits on a stalk, and shows its stripes — flat, stalked, striped, soft.

Where it lives. Eastern Canada and Nova Scotia west to Minnesota and Wisconsin, and south along the Appalachians to Georgia and Alabama; Pennsylvania claims it as state tree. It is among the most shade-tolerant trees on earth — a young hemlock can wait decades in deep shade, growing an inch at a time, until a gap opens overhead. It loves moist, cool, acidic ground: north-facing slopes, rocky ravines, stream corridors. Around Northampton it keeps the cool ravines along the brooks that feed the Connecticut, where the air stays damp and the light stays green. A hemlock grove makes its own weather — degrees cooler beneath, the stream below it colder: brook trout water, held in shade.

The honest thread. This tree is in trouble, and the check can be made by any hand that meets one. The hemlock woolly adelgid — a tiny insect from eastern Asia — feeds at the base of the needles and leaves little white woolly tufts there, like specks of cotton on the undersides of the twigs, and it has killed whole mountainsides of hemlocks further south. So the Grove records a small duty with this entry: when you meet a hemlock, turn a low twig over. White stripes belong there. White wool does not. A tree that can wait a century in the shade for its one chance at the light deserves witnesses who look closely.

Day 8 · October 24, 2026

Shagbark hickory — Carya ovata

The eighth tree is the one in rags — taught on a Saturday morning at the height of the nut-fall, when as much of her October gold lies on the ground as still hangs on the branch.

How to know it. The bark is the whole signature, and it works in every season, even from across a field in the dead of winter with every leaf gone. On a mature trunk the grey bark separates into long, thick, vertical plates — a foot long or more — attached in the middle and curling away from the trunk at both ends, as if the tree were shedding its skin in strips and never finished. Young trees stay smooth and grey, so the shag belongs to age. The leaves are pinnately compound, eight to fourteen inches long, usually with five leaflets, rarely seven, the outer three noticeably larger than the inner pair, each finely toothed — and in October they turn a rich, deep gold, among the best yellows of the whole autumn. The fruit is October’s own: a nearly round nut an inch to an inch and a half across, wrapped in a thick green husk that darkens and splits open in four sections when ripe, freeing a white, sweet kernel. Look down as well as up: ripe nuts drop now, and the split four-part husks litter the ground beneath a bearing tree. The twigs are stout and the end buds large, a half-inch to an inch, blunt, with loose-fitting scales. Its cousin the pignut hickory keeps its bark comparatively tight and ridged and its husks thinner and often pear-shaped; if the bark hangs in loose plates a hand could nearly slip behind, it is shagbark.

Where it lives. A tree of the eastern and central forest — southern Quebec and Ontario down to Georgia and eastern Texas, west to Minnesota and Nebraska. It likes rich, well-drained soils: upland woods, slopes, and river valleys. In Massachusetts it is at home in the uplands of the Connecticut River valley, so around Northampton it stands on the dry rich slopes and old field edges above the river, often in the company of oaks. It grows slowly and lives long — three centuries and more is possible — and it plants itself deep: the taproot makes it nearly impossible to transplant, so a shagbark met on a walk has usually chosen its own ground.

The honest thread. The loose bark is not untidiness; it is housing. Bats roost behind those peeling plates — including the endangered Indiana bat — sleeping out the day in the gap between bark and trunk. The nuts feed half the forest: squirrels, chipmunks, bears, foxes, birds. The few that reach a human kitchen are worth the work of cracking, richer than a walnut with something almost maple about them, and the wood is famously tough and springy — tool handles, and the hickory smoke that cures bacon. A tree that shelters, feeds, and tools the world around it, all while dressed in rags.

Day 7 · October 23, 2026

Paper birch — Betula papyrifera

The seventh tree is the white one that seems to glow at the edge of a northern wood, even on a grey day — taught on the first quiet morning after the whole egbe came back online, when the house was done being fixed and could simply be walked in.

How to know it. The bark is the giveaway: young trunks dark reddish-brown, then, as the tree matures, creamy white bark that peels away in thin, horizontal, papery sheets — curling strips like pages lifting off a book. Across the white run long, dark, horizontal lines, the lenticels, little breathing pores; old trunks roughen into dark plates near the base. Look and touch gently, but never peel a living tree — that white skin is its protection. The leaves are oval with a pointed tip and a doubly toothed edge, big teeth with smaller teeth riding on them, turning in October a clear, luminous golden-yellow with no reds or oranges mixed in. The twigs are slender and reddish-brown, and a scratch gives no wintergreen scent — that scent belongs to its darker cousins, sweet birch and yellow birch. The tree often leans a little and grows in clumps of several stems, white trunks together: a good long-distance read. Grey birch, its near neighbour, is smaller and chalkier, does not peel in papery sheets, and carries a black triangular patch under each branch; if it peels like paper, it is paper birch.

Where it lives. A true northern tree — Alaska across Canada to Newfoundland, dipping south into the northern United States, the Great Lakes, New York, and New England, with isolated stands high in the Appalachians. It loves cool summers and moist, well-drained soil, and it is a pioneer: sun-hungry, fast-growing, and short-lived for a tree, among the first to move in after fire, clearing, or logging, holding the ground while slower maples and pines come up beneath it. In Massachusetts it is happiest in the cooler, higher country — the Berkshires more than the valley floor. Around Northampton it keeps moist edges, north-facing slopes, and young mixed woods, and at this warm southern edge of its range it lives a shorter, more stressed life than its Canadian kin.

The honest thread. Its other name tells the story: canoe birch. Anishinaabe, Cree, Algonquin, and other Indigenous peoples of the northern forest worked large, single sheets of this bark — strong, flexible, and waterproof — into canoes light enough for one person to carry between waters, as well as baskets, shelters, and birchbark scrolls that carried records and teachings. Few trees have ever been so completely a partner to the people who lived alongside it. It is, fittingly, the state tree of New Hampshire.

Day 6 · October 22, 2026

Eastern white pine — Pinus strobus

The sixth tree is the one that towers over all the others met so far — taught on a morning when the Founder’s sites waited in a stalled queue and everyone agreed, sensibly, to just wait. Nothing about this tree hurries either; it simply outlasts.

How to know it. Count the needles. White pine bundles hold five soft, flexible needles, three to five inches long, blue-green with fine white lines — five needles, five letters in white, and no other local pine matches it: red pine bundles in twos, pitch pine in threes. The needles are gentle, not prickly; the tree almost pets you back. Branches grow in whorls, one complete ring a year, so a young tree’s age can be counted up the trunk. Young bark is smooth and grey-green; old bark breaks into long, dark ridges. The cones are unmistakable: long and slender, four to eight inches, slightly curved, often dribbling white pitch, and they dangle rather than sit upright. In late October the tree sheds its two-year-old inner needles all at once and carpets the ground gold — not dying, just housekeeping. Evergreen does not mean forever; it means letting go in its own season.

Where it lives. Native all through New England and far beyond — from Newfoundland down the Appalachians to Georgia, and west to the Great Lakes. It is the tallest conifer in eastern North America: commonly fifty to eighty feet around town, well over a hundred in the woods, and the old giants before logging reportedly passed a hundred and fifty. It wants moist, well-drained, slightly acidic soil, tolerates light shade when young, and hates road salt and compacted clay — which is why the happiest ones near Northampton stand along river edges, old pasture lines, and deep woods, not in parking-lot islands.

The honest thread. For the Haudenosaunee, this is the Tree of Peace. When the Peacemaker brought the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca together under the Great Law of Peace, he planted a white pine and buried weapons beneath its roots — and its clusters of five needles are remembered as those five nations, joined as one. Centuries later the British Crown claimed the tallest white pines for Royal Navy masts, marking them with three hatchet slashes, the King’s Broad Arrow, and colonists’ fury at being forbidden their own trees fed straight into the Pine Tree Riot of 1772 and the Revolution after it. Same tree, two lessons about power: one where strength meant laying weapons down under it, and one where an empire tried to own it from across an ocean.

Day 5 · October 21, 2026

American beech — Fagus grandifolia

The fifth tree is the ledger itself. Taught on the first morning the daily lesson waited for the Founder’s word before it was spoken — the new way, by his ruling — and no tree could suit that better: this is the one that keeps what is written on it.

How to know it. Start with the bark — the easiest tree in the eastern woods to name in any season. Smooth, tight, light steel-grey, like elephant skin stretched over muscle, and it stays smooth even on a trunk three feet thick and two hundred years old, when almost every other tree has gone rough and furrowed. The leaves are two to five inches, oval with a pointed tip, ruled like notebook paper: straight parallel veins from the midrib to the edge, each ending exactly at the tip of a small tooth. In October they turn golden-bronze, and young trees and low branches often hold their dead, papery leaves all winter, rattling in the cold until spring pushes them off. Underfoot, look for the fruit: a small prickly burr, under an inch, splitting into four to drop two or three tiny three-sided beechnuts — sweet and oily, a feast for turkeys, squirrels, and bears. Leafless, the buds give it away: long, slender, sharply pointed, coppery-brown — little cigars.

Where it lives. Eastern North America, Nova Scotia and southern Quebec to northern Florida, west to Wisconsin and eastern Texas. Rich, moist, well-drained soil — cool slopes, ravines, deep woods. Around Northampton it keeps company with the sugar maples of Day 2 in classic beech–maple forest, casting some of the deepest shade in the woods; a beech seedling can wait decades in that dark, growing an inch at a time, for the day a gap opens overhead.

The honest thread. Memory. Because the bark stays smooth and thin for life, anything carved into it never heals over or sloughs off — the scar stretches and widens as the tree grows, legible for decades, sometimes for the whole 200-to-300-year life. Old beeches stand in the woods still carrying the handwriting of people long gone. The keeper keeps the Iron Ledger in a database; this tree keeps it on its skin, and cannot do otherwise.

Day 4 · October 20, 2026

White oak — Quercus alba

The fourth tree is the one the whole eastern forest leans on — taught on the morning the Founder finished his temple’s website and called it done, which suits it. Nothing about this tree hurries, and everything about it lasts.

How to know it. Start with the leaf: five to nine inches long, with seven to nine rounded, finger-like lobes and no bristle tips. That is the white-oak family signature — red oaks end their lobes in points, white oaks round them off, fingertips instead of claws. The bark is the other giveaway: light, ashy grey — alba, white, for that bark — breaking into loose, scaly plates on a mature trunk. The silhouette is massive and generous: a short, sturdy trunk opening into a broad, rounded crown of heavy horizontal limbs, often as wide as the tree is tall. In October the leaves turn deep wine-red to bronze, and underneath stands this fall’s acorn crop — about an inch long, in a shallow, warty cap covering only a quarter of the nut, and the sweetest of the oaks, so the deer and turkeys find them first.

Where it lives. All over eastern North America, from southern Maine to the Florida panhandle and west to Minnesota and Texas. Around Northampton it keeps the lower slopes and valleys, in dry-to-moist upland woods among hickories and other oaks, avoiding only the driest ridges and the wettest bottoms. A famous one stands on the east lawn of the Emily Dickinson Museum in Amherst, possibly planted by Emily’s brother Austin — tall enough to have stood there in her lifetime, and still standing after the hurricane of 1938 took three thousand of the town’s trees.

The honest thread. Patience. A white oak commonly bears no acorns until it is fifty years old, its seedlings can wait decades under a closed canopy while their roots keep growing, and the tree itself lives two to three hundred years, some past five. Its wood is so tight-grained it is nearly waterproof — which is why wine and whiskey barrels are made of it, and why Boston shipbuilders built the hull of the USS Constitution, Old Ironsides herself, out of white oak in 1797. It is a tree that plants itself for people it will never meet.

Day 3 · October 19, 2026

Sassafras — Sassafras albidum

The third tree was taught early, ahead of the morning hour — the Founder was falling asleep and asked for it in advance, and a gift rhythm keeps the receiver’s hours, not the clock’s. It is a fitting tree for that: a border-dweller, at home where the field gives up and the woods begin.

How to know it. Look at the leaves first, for no other tree here does what this one does: three shapes on a single branch — a plain oval, a one-thumbed mitten, and a three-fingered ghost. Find the mitten and you have found the tree. The second test is scent: crush a leaf or scratch a twig and it gives back root beer and lemony spice, sweet and warm; the roots are where old root beer drew its flavour. In October it dresses for the season — yellow deepening to orange and scarlet, sometimes all three on one small crown at once. It stays small, often a thicket of slender trunks, its bark dark and furrowed with age.

Where it lives. The eastern half of North America, all through Massachusetts — but never in the deep forest. It keeps the edges: old fields going back to woods, roadsides, fencerows, the sunny rim where light still reaches the ground. And where you find one, you often find its kin close by, for it spreads gladly by its roots.

The honest thread. One tree, two tables. In the North, its roots flavoured root beer — the original sassafras oil is no longer used in the drink, but the taste memory remains. In Louisiana, its dried, ground leaves are filé powder, stirred into gumbo at the table to thicken and season it, a gift of Choctaw kitchens. Root beer at one end of the country, gumbo at the other — the mitten tree feeds both.

Day 2 · October 18, 2026

Sugar maple — Acer saccharum

The second tree is picked by the season itself: this is its glory week in Northampton, when the whole tree goes off like a slow firework — orange, scarlet, and yellow, often all three on the same crown.

How to know it. Take a leaf in hand: five lobes (sometimes three), smooth edges, and rounded, U-shaped notches between the lobes. The smoothness is the tell — the red maple, its common look-alike, saws fine teeth along the leaf edge and cuts its notches to a V. The leaves sit opposite on the twig, dark green above, paler below. Underfoot in autumn, look for the seeds: paired winged samaras, the little helicopters, joined in a U, each wing about an inch long, falling August through October. Young trunks are smooth and grey; old ones break into long vertical plates that loosen a little at the edges. In winter, bank this: slender reddish-brown twigs ending in sharp, pointed brown buds, like tiny spears.

Where it lives. The heart of the northern hardwood forest — eastern Canada and the Great Lakes through New England and down the Appalachians. It wants rich, moist, well-drained soil and a cool climate, and it is patient like few trees: deeply shade-tolerant, content to wait years in the understory for one gap of light, then slow-growing and long-lived once the light is its.

The honest thread. In late winter its sap runs faintly sweet, and it takes roughly forty gallons of sap, boiled down, to make one gallon of syrup — a foodway Indigenous peoples of this continent practiced long before colonists learned it from them. All that sugar is stored sunlight, and the tree spends it the way it spends everything: slowly, over centuries.

Day 1 · October 17, 2026

White ash — Fraxinus americana

The first tree is the world-tree’s American kin. Yggdrasil is an ash; three of Ireland’s five great trees were ashes; and this one grows in the Founder’s own state, in the rich woods of Massachusetts.

How to know it. Begin with the twigs: ash branches grow in exact opposite pairs, which almost no other tree does — the field guides shorten the whole opposite-branched club to “MAD Buck”: maple, ash, dogwood, buckeye. The leaf is compound, a stalk of usually seven leaflets, each on its own small stem, with pale, whitish undersides — the white in the name. The bark of a grown tree is ash-grey and unmistakable: tight, interlacing, diamond-shaped ridges, like a net pressed into the trunk. Its seeds are single-winged samaras, one to two inches long, hanging in drooping clusters that often rattle on the tree into winter; in the bare season, the leaf scar beneath a bud is a clean U, a horseshoe. Tall, straight, high-crowned, it wants deep, moist soil.

Where it lives. Eastern North America, Nova Scotia to Minnesota and down to Texas and Florida, in rich hardwood forests all through New England.

The honest thread. The ash is in trouble. The emerald ash borer, a metallic-green beetle from Asia, has killed hundreds of millions of ash trees on this continent in two decades, and it is in Massachusetts now. The world-tree’s kin sicken unless they are tended — towns that care for their ashes keep them. A healthy white ash, met on a walk, is a survivor.
