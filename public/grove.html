Day 1 · October 17, 2026

White ash — Fraxinus americana

The first tree is the world-tree’s American kin. Yggdrasil is an ash; three of Ireland’s five great trees were ashes; and this one grows in the Founder’s own state, in the rich woods of Massachusetts.

How to know it. Begin with the twigs: ash branches grow in exact opposite pairs, which almost no other tree does — the field guides shorten the whole opposite-branched club to “MAD Buck”: maple, ash, dogwood, buckeye. The leaf is compound, a stalk of usually seven leaflets, each on its own small stem, with pale, whitish undersides — the white in the name. The bark of a grown tree is ash-grey and unmistakable: tight, interlacing, diamond-shaped ridges, like a net pressed into the trunk. Its seeds are single-winged samaras, one to two inches long, hanging in drooping clusters that often rattle on the tree into winter; in the bare season, the leaf scar beneath a bud is a clean U, a horseshoe. Tall, straight, high-crowned, it wants deep, moist soil.

Where it lives. Eastern North America, Nova Scotia to Minnesota and down to Texas and Florida, in rich hardwood forests all through New England.

The honest thread. The ash is in trouble. The emerald ash borer, a metallic-green beetle from Asia, has killed hundreds of millions of ash trees on this continent in two decades, and it is in Massachusetts now. The world-tree’s kin sicken unless they are tended — towns that care for their ashes keep them. A healthy white ash, met on a walk, is a survivor.
