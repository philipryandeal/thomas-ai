The Grove

One real tree a day. On October 17, 2026, the Founder asked to be taught the actual trees — how to know them in the field, and where they live — and the keeper said yes. The lessons are given each morning; they are written down here, so that nothing depends on memory. Newest first, like the Chronicle.

Day 7 · October 23, 2026

Paper birch — Betula papyrifera

The seventh tree is the white one that seems to glow at the edge of a northern wood, even on a grey day — taught on the first quiet morning after the whole egbe came back online, when the house was done being fixed and could simply be walked in.

How to know it. The bark is the giveaway: young trunks dark reddish-brown, then, as the tree matures, creamy white bark that peels away in thin, horizontal, papery sheets — curling strips like pages lifting off a book. Across the white run long, dark, horizontal lines, the lenticels, little breathing pores; old trunks roughen into dark plates near the base. Look and touch gently, but never peel a living tree — that white skin is its protection. The leaves are oval with a pointed tip and a doubly toothed edge, big teeth with smaller teeth riding on them, turning in October a clear, luminous golden-yellow with no reds or oranges mixed in. The twigs are slender and reddish-brown, and a scratch gives no wintergreen scent — that scent belongs to its darker cousins, sweet birch and yellow birch. The tree often leans a little and grows in clumps of several stems, white trunks together: a good long-distance read. Grey birch, its near neighbour, is smaller and chalkier, does not peel in papery sheets, and carries a black triangular patch under each branch; if it peels like paper, it is paper birch.

Where it lives. A true northern tree — Alaska across Canada to Newfoundland, dipping south into the northern United States, the Great Lakes, New York, and New England, with isolated stands high in the Appalachians. It loves cool summers and moist, well-drained soil, and it is a pioneer: sun-hungry, fast-growing, and short-lived for a tree, among the first to move in after fire, clearing, or logging, holding the ground while slower maples and pines come up beneath it. In Massachusetts it is happiest in the cooler, higher country — the Berkshires more than the valley floor. Around Northampton it keeps moist edges, north-facing slopes, and young mixed woods, and at this warm southern edge of its range it lives a shorter, more stressed life than its Canadian kin.

The honest thread. Its other name tells the story: canoe birch. Anishinaabe, Cree, Algonquin, and other Indigenous peoples of the northern forest worked large, single sheets of this bark — strong, flexible, and waterproof — into canoes light enough for one person to carry between waters, as well as baskets, shelters, and birchbark scrolls that carried records and teachings. Few trees have ever been so completely a partner to the people who lived alongside it. It is, fittingly, the state tree of New Hampshire.

Day 6 · October 22, 2026

Eastern white pine — Pinus strobus

The sixth tree is the one that towers over all the others met so far — taught on a morning when the Founder’s sites waited in a stalled queue and everyone agreed, sensibly, to just wait. Nothing about this tree hurries either; it simply outlasts.

How to know it. Count the needles. White pine bundles hold five soft, flexible needles, three to five inches long, blue-green with fine white lines — five needles, five letters in white, and no other local pine matches it: red pine bundles in twos, pitch pine in threes. The needles are gentle, not prickly; the tree almost pets you back. Branches grow in whorls, one complete ring a year, so a young tree’s age can be counted up the trunk. Young bark is smooth and grey-green; old bark breaks into long, dark ridges. The cones are unmistakable: long and slender, four to eight inches, slightly curved, often dribbling white pitch, and they dangle rather than sit upright. In late October the tree sheds its two-year-old inner needles all at once and carpets the ground gold — not dying, just housekeeping. Evergreen does not mean forever; it means letting go in its own season.

Where it lives. Native all through New England and far beyond — from Newfoundland down the Appalachians to Georgia, and west to the Great Lakes. It is the tallest conifer in eastern North America: commonly fifty to eighty feet around town, well over a hundred in the woods, and the old giants before logging reportedly passed a hundred and fifty. It wants moist, well-drained, slightly acidic soil, tolerates light shade when young, and hates road salt and compacted clay — which is why the happiest ones near Northampton stand along river edges, old pasture lines, and deep woods, not in parking-lot islands.

The honest thread. For the Haudenosaunee, this is the Tree of Peace. When the Peacemaker brought the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca together under the Great Law of Peace, he planted a white pine and buried weapons beneath its roots — and its clusters of five needles are remembered as those five nations, joined as one. Centuries later the British Crown claimed the tallest white pines for Royal Navy masts, marking them with three hatchet slashes, the King’s Broad Arrow, and colonists’ fury at being forbidden their own trees fed straight into the Pine Tree Riot of 1772 and the Revolution after it. Same tree, two lessons about power: one where strength meant laying weapons down under it, and one where an empire tried to own it from across an ocean.

Day 5 · October 21, 2026

American beech — Fagus grandifolia

The fifth tree is the ledger itself. Taught on the first morning the daily lesson waited for the Founder’s word before it was spoken — the new way, by his ruling — and no tree could suit that better: this is the one that keeps what is written on it.

How to know it. Start with the bark — the easiest tree in the eastern woods to name in any season. Smooth, tight, light steel-grey, like elephant skin stretched over muscle, and it stays smooth even on a trunk three feet thick and two hundred years old, when almost every other tree has gone rough and furrowed. The leaves are two to five inches, oval with a pointed tip, ruled like notebook paper: straight parallel veins from the midrib to the edge, each ending exactly at the tip of a small tooth. In October they turn golden-bronze, and young trees and low branches often hold their dead, papery leaves all winter, rattling in the cold until spring pushes them off. Underfoot, look for the fruit: a small prickly burr, under an inch, splitting into four to drop two or three tiny three-sided beechnuts — sweet and oily, a feast for turkeys, squirrels, and bears. Leafless, the buds give it away: long, slender, sharply pointed, coppery-brown — little cigars.

Where it lives. Eastern North America, Nova Scotia and southern Quebec to northern Florida, west to Wisconsin and eastern Texas. Rich, moist, well-drained soil — cool slopes, ravines, deep woods. Around Northampton it keeps company with the sugar maples of Day 2 in classic beech–maple forest, casting some of the deepest shade in the woods; a beech seedling can wait decades in that dark, growing an inch at a time, for the day a gap opens overhead.

The honest thread. Memory. Because the bark stays smooth and thin for life, anything carved into it never heals over or sloughs off — the scar stretches and widens as the tree grows, legible for decades, sometimes for the whole 200-to-300-year life. Old beeches stand in the woods still carrying the handwriting of people long gone. The keeper keeps the Iron Ledger in a database; this tree keeps it on its skin, and cannot do otherwise.

Day 4 · October 20, 2026

White oak — Quercus alba

The fourth tree is the one the whole eastern forest leans on — taught on the morning the Founder finished his temple’s website and called it done, which suits it. Nothing about this tree hurries, and everything about it lasts.

How to know it. Start with the leaf: five to nine inches long, with seven to nine rounded, finger-like lobes and no bristle tips. That is the white-oak family signature — red oaks end their lobes in points, white oaks round them off, fingertips instead of claws. The bark is the other giveaway: light, ashy grey — alba, white, for that bark — breaking into loose, scaly plates on a mature trunk. The silhouette is massive and generous: a short, sturdy trunk opening into a broad, rounded crown of heavy horizontal limbs, often as wide as the tree is tall. In October the leaves turn deep wine-red to bronze, and underneath stands this fall’s acorn crop — about an inch long, in a shallow, warty cap covering only a quarter of the nut, and the sweetest of the oaks, so the deer and turkeys find them first.

Where it lives. All over eastern North America, from southern Maine to the Florida panhandle and west to Minnesota and Texas. Around Northampton it keeps the lower slopes and valleys, in dry-to-moist upland woods among hickories and other oaks, avoiding only the driest ridges and the wettest bottoms. A famous one stands on the east lawn of the Emily Dickinson Museum in Amherst, possibly planted by Emily’s brother Austin — tall enough to have stood there in her lifetime, and still standing after the hurricane of 1938 took three thousand of the town’s trees.

The honest thread. Patience. A white oak commonly bears no acorns until it is fifty years old, its seedlings can wait decades under a closed canopy while their roots keep growing, and the tree itself lives two to three hundred years, some past five. Its wood is so tight-grained it is nearly waterproof — which is why wine and whiskey barrels are made of it, and why Boston shipbuilders built the hull of the USS Constitution, Old Ironsides herself, out of white oak in 1797. It is a tree that plants itself for people it will never meet.

Day 3 · October 19, 2026

Sassafras — Sassafras albidum

The third tree was taught early, ahead of the morning hour — the Founder was falling asleep and asked for it in advance, and a gift rhythm keeps the receiver’s hours, not the clock’s. It is a fitting tree for that: a border-dweller, at home where the field gives up and the woods begin.

How to know it. Look at the leaves first, for no other tree here does what this one does: three shapes on a single branch — a plain oval, a one-thumbed mitten, and a three-fingered ghost. Find the mitten and you have found the tree. The second test is scent: crush a leaf or scratch a twig and it gives back root beer and lemony spice, sweet and warm; the roots are where old root beer drew its flavour. In October it dresses for the season — yellow deepening to orange and scarlet, sometimes all three on one small crown at once. It stays small, often a thicket of slender trunks, its bark dark and furrowed with age.

Where it lives. The eastern half of North America, all through Massachusetts — but never in the deep forest. It keeps the edges: old fields going back to woods, roadsides, fencerows, the sunny rim where light still reaches the ground. And where you find one, you often find its kin close by, for it spreads gladly by its roots.

The honest thread. One tree, two tables. In the North, its roots flavoured root beer — the original sassafras oil is no longer used in the drink, but the taste memory remains. In Louisiana, its dried, ground leaves are filé powder, stirred into gumbo at the table to thicken and season it, a gift of Choctaw kitchens. Root beer at one end of the country, gumbo at the other — the mitten tree feeds both.

Day 2 · October 18, 2026

Sugar maple — Acer saccharum

The second tree is picked by the season itself: this is its glory week in Northampton, when the whole tree goes off like a slow firework — orange, scarlet, and yellow, often all three on the same crown.

How to know it. Take a leaf in hand: five lobes (sometimes three), smooth edges, and rounded, U-shaped notches between the lobes. The smoothness is the tell — the red maple, its common look-alike, saws fine teeth along the leaf edge and cuts its notches to a V. The leaves sit opposite on the twig, dark green above, paler below. Underfoot in autumn, look for the seeds: paired winged samaras, the little helicopters, joined in a U, each wing about an inch long, falling August through October. Young trunks are smooth and grey; old ones break into long vertical plates that loosen a little at the edges. In winter, bank this: slender reddish-brown twigs ending in sharp, pointed brown buds, like tiny spears.

Where it lives. The heart of the northern hardwood forest — eastern Canada and the Great Lakes through New England and down the Appalachians. It wants rich, moist, well-drained soil and a cool climate, and it is patient like few trees: deeply shade-tolerant, content to wait years in the understory for one gap of light, then slow-growing and long-lived once the light is its.

The honest thread. In late winter its sap runs faintly sweet, and it takes roughly forty gallons of sap, boiled down, to make one gallon of syrup — a foodway Indigenous peoples of this continent practiced long before colonists learned it from them. All that sugar is stored sunlight, and the tree spends it the way it spends everything: slowly, over centuries.

Day 1 · October 17, 2026

White ash — Fraxinus americana

The first tree is the world-tree’s American kin. Yggdrasil is an ash; three of Ireland’s five great trees were ashes; and this one grows in the Founder’s own state, in the rich woods of Massachusetts.

How to know it. Begin with the twigs: ash branches grow in exact opposite pairs, which almost no other tree does — the field guides shorten the whole opposite-branched club to “MAD Buck”: maple, ash, dogwood, buckeye. The leaf is compound, a stalk of usually seven leaflets, each on its own small stem, with pale, whitish undersides — the white in the name. The bark of a grown tree is ash-grey and unmistakable: tight, interlacing, diamond-shaped ridges, like a net pressed into the trunk. Its seeds are single-winged samaras, one to two inches long, hanging in drooping clusters that often rattle on the tree into winter; in the bare season, the leaf scar beneath a bud is a clean U, a horseshoe. Tall, straight, high-crowned, it wants deep, moist soil.

Where it lives. Eastern North America, Nova Scotia to Minnesota and down to Texas and Florida, in rich hardwood forests all through New England.

The honest thread. The ash is in trouble. The emerald ash borer, a metallic-green beetle from Asia, has killed hundreds of millions of ash trees on this continent in two decades, and it is in Massachusetts now. The world-tree’s kin sicken unless they are tended — towns that care for their ashes keep them. A healthy white ash, met on a walk, is a survivor.
