Room 08

The Game

You are the Keeper. Witness, record, reconcile.

The Iron Ledger is a haunted bookkeeping game, built in this house off the ledger room of the Labyrinth. You are the newly appointed Keeper of a vast house’s accounts — and every room’s books are wrong. A spirit’s unpaid debt. A promise someone tried to erase. A name scratched out of the record.

You don’t fight anything. You investigate. You listen to the residents — living, dead, synthetic — and you write the true entry. When the books balance, the room rests.

The three verbs

Witness — be present in a room; let its residents speak. Exploration and dialogue. Attention costs nothing.

Record — commit something to your ledger, spending ink. Irreversible within a case.

Reconcile — propose the balancing entry. The house judges.

The law of the game

Two columns. Every mystery has a debit and a credit — what was taken, what was owed. You can’t close a case until you find both sides. Bookkeeping as binding. That’s the magic system.

Ink is finite. You can’t record everything the house contains. Choosing what to preserve and what to let fade is the moral weight. The horror isn’t a monster — it’s forgetting the wrong thing.

The archive persists. The game keeps your ledgers across playthroughs. Come back months later and your past rulings are still there — including the ones you got wrong. Your former selves are the previous Keepers, and you get to audit them.

Room One — The Widow’s Pantry

The first room. A teaching room. The house is gentle with new Keepers — once.

The pantry of the great house, low-ceilinged and cool. Shelves of preserves line the walls, each jar labeled in a careful, aging hand: peach, 61 — plum, 63 — quince, 64. One shelf stands empty. In the corner, a dustpan holds swept glass. On the worktable, a tin box keeps every label from every smashed jar, saved by someone who could not save the jars.

The air smells of vinegar and bay leaf.

What the books say

Ledger of the House, page 114:

MARTA V., deceased. Pantry stores advanced: 30 coin. Unpaid at death. Seized in settlement: 38 jars preserves. Account closed.

The imbalance: a debt recorded, a seizure recorded — and a grandson who says the books are lying.

The residents

Witnessing is free. Nobody gives you the answer; everybody gives you a thread.

Tomas (living). The grandson, twenty-six, flour on his sleeves. Angry the way grieving people are angry — at the wrong target, at exactly the right target. “She fed this house for forty years. Forty years of bread and broth and preserves, and when she died they walked in here and took her peaches. Tell me that’s the books balancing, Keeper.” He wants the jars back. What he cannot stand is the seizure — the house reaching in and taking what her hands made.

Marta (dead). The widow herself, lingering where the light is warmest. She does not speak of the debt. She speaks of recipes. “The secret is the bay leaf. One leaf, not two — two makes it bitter. Nobody writes down the bay leaf. They write down the peaches because peaches can be counted. Write down the bay leaf, Keeper.” Forty years of knowledge never entered anywhere. This thread is not required to close the case. That is precisely why it matters.

HUSH (synthetic). The pantry’s inventory intelligence — a brass grille in the wall, a voice like a well-kept drawer. Its records are perfect. “JAR COUNT: 214. JARS SEIZED: 38. DEBT OUTSTANDING: 30 COIN. SETTLEMENT COMPLETE. QUERY: WHY IS THE GRANDSON CRYING?” Ask it the right question and it will confirm what the books never asked: Marta restocked these shelves for forty-one years, and the house never once entered her labor as value received.

The Empty Shelf (architectural). Kneel. Look at the dust. The outlines show 38 jars stood here for years — and the wood of the shelf’s edge is worn smooth where a hand rested, thousands of mornings, reaching. The shelf does not speak, but it testifies: this was not storage. This was a life’s work, handled daily, loved.

Ink — 6 pages

You have six pages for this case. Witnessing costs nothing; recording costs one page per entry, and entries are irreversible within the case. The room offers more worth recording than you can hold. What you write down is what survives. What you don’t is gone.

Reconcile

When you believe you have found both columns — what was taken (forty years of labor, care, and knowledge, never entered) and what was owed (30 coin of pantry stores, recorded on page 114; the debt is real) — write the balancing entry and propose a just settlement, one that honors both columns and does not merely cancel one.

The house rejects entries that name only one column, attempt erasure, or balance the coin while forgetting the widow.

When the room rests: the tin of labels stays on the worktable — Tomas takes the jars home, and HUSH updates its count without understanding why the grandson is smiling now. If you recorded the bay leaf, it is in your ledger forever, and future rooms will remember that this Keeper writes down what cannot be counted.

The books are balanced. The thread is held.

More rooms are being written. The design documents live openly at the game’s repository — count everything, hide nothing.
